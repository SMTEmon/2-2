# 1. Binomial Distribution & Frequency Fitting

> [!abstract] Overview & Key Concepts
> This note covers the discrete **Binomial Distribution**, including its fundamental assumptions, probability mass function (PMF), complete mathematical derivations of mean and variance, the **Binomial Recursion Relation**, fitting theoretical frequencies to observed empirical data, and critical parameter constraints.

---

## 1.1 Bernoulli Trials & Experiment Assumptions

A **Binomial Experiment** consists of repeated independent trials called **Bernoulli Trials**. For a random experiment to follow a Binomial distribution, it must satisfy **4 strict conditions**:

1. **Fixed Number of Trials ($n$):** The experiment is repeated for a fixed, predetermined number of identical trials, denoted by $n$.
2. **Two Mutually Exclusive Outcomes:** Each trial results in one of only two possible outcomes:
   - **Success ($S$)** with probability $p$
   - **Failure ($F$)** with probability $q = 1 - p$
3. **Constant Probability ($p$):** The probability of success $p$ remains constant from trial to trial.
4. **Independence:** All trials are independent of each other (the outcome of any trial has no effect on the outcome of another).

> [!info] Random Variable Definition
> If $X$ is a discrete random variable designating the total number of successes in $n$ independent Bernoulli trials, taking values $x \in \{0, 1, 2, \dots, n\}$, then $X$ is called a **Binomial Random Variable**, denoted as $X \sim B(n, p)$.

---

## 1.2 Probability Mass Function (PMF)

The probability of obtaining exactly $x$ successes in $n$ trials is given by the Binomial PMF:

$$b(x; n, p) = P(X = x) = \begin{cases} \binom{n}{x} p^x q^{n-x}, & x = 0, 1, 2, \dots, n \\ 0, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

where $\binom{n}{x} = {}^n C_x = \frac{n!}{x!(n-x)!}$ represents the number of ways to choose $x$ successes out of $n$ trials.

---

## 1.3 Mathematical Derivations of Mean and Variance

> [!note]- View Proof: Derivation of Mean $E[X] = np$
> By definition of expectation for a discrete random variable:
> 
> $$E[X] = \sum_{x=0}^{n} x \cdot b(x; n, p) = \sum_{x=0}^{n} x \cdot \binom{n}{x} p^x q^{n-x}$$
> 
> Since the term for $x = 0$ is zero:
> 
> $$E[X] = \sum_{x=1}^{n} x \cdot \frac{n!}{x!(n-x)!} p^x q^{n-x}$$
> 
> Simplifying $x \cdot \frac{n!}{x!} = \frac{n!}{(x-1)!} = \frac{n(n-1)!}{(x-1)!}$:
> 
> $$E[X] = n p \sum_{x=1}^{n} \frac{(n-1)!}{(x-1)!((n-1)-(x-1))!} p^{x-1} q^{(n-1)-(x-1)}$$
> 
> Let $y = x - 1$ and $m = n - 1$. As $x$ goes from $1$ to $n$, $y$ goes from $0$ to $m$:
> 
> $$E[X] = np \sum_{y=0}^{m} \binom{m}{y} p^y q^{m-y}$$
> 
> By the Binomial Theorem, $\sum_{y=0}^{m} \binom{m}{y} p^y q^{m-y} = (p + q)^m = 1^m = 1$.
> 
> $$\therefore E[X] = np$$

> [!note]- View Proof: Derivation of Variance $V[X] = npq$
> Variance is defined as $V[X] = E[X^2] - (E[X])^2$.
> We write $X^2 = X(X-1) + X$, so $E[X^2] = E[X(X-1)] + E[X]$.
> 
> Computing $E[X(X-1)]$:
> 
> $$E[X(X-1)] = \sum_{x=0}^{n} x(x-1) \binom{n}{x} p^x q^{n-x} = \sum_{x=2}^{n} x(x-1) \frac{n!}{x!(n-x)!} p^x q^{n-x}$$
> 
> Since $x(x-1) / x! = 1 / (x-2)!$ and $n! = n(n-1)(n-2)!$:
> 
> $$E[X(X-1)] = n(n-1) p^2 \sum_{x=2}^{n} \frac{(n-2)!}{(x-2)!((n-2)-(x-2))!} p^{x-2} q^{(n-2)-(x-2)}$$
> 
> Let $y = x - 2$ and $k = n - 2$:
> 
> $$E[X(X-1)] = n(n-1) p^2 \sum_{y=0}^{k} \binom{k}{y} p^y q^{k-y} = n(n-1) p^2 (p+q)^k = n(n-1) p^2$$
> 
> Now, substituting back into $E[X^2]$:
> 
> $$E[X^2] = n(n-1)p^2 + np = n^2 p^2 - n p^2 + np$$
> 
> Computing Variance:
> 
> $$V[X] = E[X^2] - (E[X])^2 = (n^2 p^2 - n p^2 + np) - (np)^2 = np - np^2 = np(1-p) = npq$$
> 
> $$\therefore V[X] = npq$$

## 1.4 Binomial Recursion Relation

When calculating successive probabilities $b(x; n, p)$, computing combinations repeatedly is computationally slow. We use the **Binomial Recursion Relation**:

$$b(x+1; n, p) = \left( \frac{n-x}{x+1} \right) \left( \frac{p}{q} \right) b(x; n, p)$$

> [!note]- View Proof: Binomial Recursion Formula
> We know:
> 
> $$b(x; n, p) = \frac{n!}{x!(n-x)!} p^x q^{n-x} \quad \text{--- (1)}$$
> 
> $$b(x+1; n, p) = \frac{n!}{(x+1)!(n-x-1)!} p^{x+1} q^{n-x-1} \quad \text{--- (2)}$$
> 
> Dividing equation (2) by equation (1):
> 
> $$\frac{b(x+1; n, p)}{b(x; n, p)} = \frac{\frac{n!}{(x+1)!(n-x-1)!} p^{x+1} q^{n-x-1}}{\frac{n!}{x!(n-x)!} p^x q^{n-x}}$$
> 
> $$\frac{b(x+1; n, p)}{b(x; n, p)} = \frac{x! (n-x)!}{(x+1)! (n-x-1)!} \cdot \frac{p^{x+1} q^{n-x-1}}{p^x q^{n-x}}$$
> 
> Expressing $(x+1)! = (x+1)x!$ and $(n-x)! = (n-x)(n-x-1)!$:
> 
> $$\frac{b(x+1; n, p)}{b(x; n, p)} = \frac{x! (n-x)(n-x-1)!}{(x+1)x! (n-x-1)!} \cdot \frac{p}{q} = \frac{n-x}{x+1} \cdot \frac{p}{q}$$
> 
> $$\therefore b(x+1; n, p) = \left( \frac{n-x}{x+1} \right) \left( \frac{p}{q} \right) b(x; n, p)$$

---

## 1.5 Fitting Theoretical Binomial Frequencies

To fit a theoretical Binomial distribution to empirical observed data:

1. **Calculate Sample Mean ($\bar{x}$):**
   $$\bar{x} = \frac{\sum f_i x_i}{\sum f_i}$$
2. **Estimate Parameter $p$:**
   Since $E[X] = np$, equate $\bar{x} = np \implies p = \frac{\bar{x}}{n}$, and $q = 1 - p$.
3. **Calculate Initial Probability $b(0)$:**
   $$b(0; n, p) = q^n$$
4. **Use Recursion Relation to find $b(1), b(2), \dots, b(n)$:**
   $$b(x+1) = \left(\frac{n-x}{x+1}\right)\left(\frac{p}{q}\right) b(x)$$
5. **Calculate Expected Frequencies ($E_x$):**
   $$E_x = N \cdot b(x; n, p) \quad \text{where } N = \sum f_i \text{ (total frequency)}$$

## 1.6 Worked Examples

### Problem 1: Fitting Binomial Frequencies to Coin Toss Experiment
**Question:** Five coins are tossed simultaneously, and the experiment is repeated $N = 200$ times. The observed frequencies for the number of heads obtained are shown below:

| No. of Heads ($x$) | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | Total ($N$) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Observed Frequency ($f$)** | 12 | 56 | 74 | 39 | 18 | 1 | **200** |

Fit a theoretical Binomial distribution to this data using the recursion relation and compute expected frequencies.

> [!note]- View Complete Solution
> **Step 1: Compute Sample Mean $\bar{x}$**
> 
> $$\bar{x} = \frac{\sum f x}{\sum f} = \frac{(0 \times 12) + (1 \times 56) + (2 \times 74) + (3 \times 39) + (4 \times 18) + (5 \times 1)}{200}$$
> 
> $$\bar{x} = \frac{0 + 56 + 148 + 117 + 72 + 5}{200} = \frac{398}{200} = 1.99$$
> 
> **Step 2: Estimate Parameter $p$**
> Equating sample mean to Binomial mean $np$:
> 
> $$np = 1.99 \implies 5p = 1.99 \implies p = \frac{1.99}{5} = 0.398$$
> 
> $$q = 1 - p = 1 - 0.398 = 0.602$$
> 
> Ratio $\frac{p}{q} = \frac{0.398}{0.602} \approx 0.66113$
> 
> **Step 3: Initial Term $b(0)$**
> 
> $$b(0; 5, 0.398) = \binom{5}{0} (0.398)^0 (0.602)^5 = (0.602)^5 = 0.07907$$
> 
> **Step 4: Recursive Computation of Probabilities**
> - For $x = 0$:
>   $$b(1) = \left(\frac{5-0}{0+1}\right)\left(\frac{0.398}{0.602}\right) b(0) = 5 \times (0.66113) \times 0.07907 = 0.26136$$
> - For $x = 1$:
>   $$b(2) = \left(\frac{5-1}{1+1}\right)\left(\frac{0.398}{0.602}\right) b(1) = 2 \times (0.66113) \times 0.26136 = 0.34559$$
> - For $x = 2$:
>   $$b(3) = \left(\frac{5-2}{2+1}\right)\left(\frac{0.398}{0.602}\right) b(2) = 1 \times (0.66113) \times 0.34559 = 0.22847$$
> - For $x = 3$:
>   $$b(4) = \left(\frac{5-3}{3+1}\right)\left(\frac{0.398}{0.602}\right) b(3) = 0.5 \times (0.66113) \times 0.22847 = 0.07553$$
> - For $x = 4$:
>   $$b(5) = \left(\frac{5-4}{4+1}\right)\left(\frac{0.398}{0.602}\right) b(4) = 0.2 \times (0.66113) \times 0.07553 = 0.00998$$
> 
> **Step 5: Compute Expected Frequencies $E_x = 200 \times b(x)$**
> - $E_0 = 200 \times 0.07907 = 15.81 \approx 16$
> - $E_1 = 200 \times 0.26136 = 52.27 \approx 52$
> - $E_2 = 200 \times 0.34559 = 69.12 \approx 69$
> - $E_3 = 200 \times 0.22847 = 45.69 \approx 46$
> - $E_4 = 200 \times 0.07553 = 15.11 \approx 15$
> - $E_5 = 200 \times 0.00998 = 2.00 \approx 2$
> 
> **Comparison Table:**
> 
> | $X = x$ | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | Total |
> |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
> | **Observed Frequency ($f$)** | 12 | 56 | 74 | 39 | 18 | 1 | **200** |
> | **Expected Frequency ($E_x$)** | 16 | 52 | 69 | 46 | 15 | 2 | **200** |

### Problem 2: Biased Coin Experiment Parameter Derivation
**Question:** In an experiment tossing 4 coins, the observed weighted mean number of heads is $\bar{x} = 2.6$. Derive the probability parameter $p$.

> [!note]- View Solution
> Using $E[X] = np$:
> 
> $$np = 2.6 \implies 4p = 2.6 \implies p = \frac{2.6}{4} = 0.65$$
> 
> Thus $q = 1 - 0.65 = 0.35$.

### Problem 3: Traffic Flow Problem
**Question:** The probability that a truck entering Dhaka city originates outside Dhaka is $p = 0.25$. If 5 trucks are randomly inspected ($n = 5$), find the probability that at least 3 trucks are from outside Dhaka.

> [!note]- View Solution
> We need $P(X \ge 3) = P(X = 3) + P(X = 4) + P(X = 5)$ for $n = 5, p = 0.25, q = 0.75$.
> 
> $$P(X = 3) = \binom{5}{3} (0.25)^3 (0.75)^2 = 10 \times 0.015625 \times 0.5625 = 0.08789$$
> 
> $$P(X = 4) = \binom{5}{4} (0.25)^4 (0.75)^1 = 5 \times 0.003906 \times 0.75 = 0.01465$$
> 
> $$P(X = 5) = \binom{5}{5} (0.25)^5 (0.75)^0 = 1 \times 0.0009765 = 0.0009765$$
> 
> $$P(X \ge 3) = 0.08789 + 0.01465 + 0.00098 = 0.10352$$

### Problem 4: Serum Inoculation Protection (Slide Problem)
**Question:** It is known that $90\%$ of mice inoculated with a serum are protected against a certain disease. If 5 inoculated mice are exposed to the disease ($n = 5$), what is the probability that:
1. Exactly 3 mice contract the disease?
2. At least 2 mice contract the disease?

> [!note]- View Complete Solution
> Let $X$ be the number of mice that **contract the disease**.
> - Probability of contracting the disease (success in this context): $p = 1 - 0.90 = 0.10$
> - Probability of being protected: $q = 0.90$
> - Number of trials: $n = 5$
> - Random variable $X \sim B(5, 0.10)$
> 
> #### Part (i): Exactly 3 contract the disease ($X = 3$)
> $$P(X = 3) = \binom{5}{3} (0.10)^3 (0.90)^2 = 10 \times (0.001) \times (0.81) = 0.0081$$
> 
> #### Part (ii): At least 2 contract the disease ($X \ge 2$)
> Using the complement rule:
> $$P(X \ge 2) = 1 - [P(X = 0) + P(X = 1)]$$
> 
> $$P(X = 0) = \binom{5}{0} (0.10)^0 (0.90)^5 = (0.90)^5 = 0.59049$$
> 
> $$P(X = 1) = \binom{5}{1} (0.10)^1 (0.90)^4 = 5 \times 0.10 \times 0.6561 = 0.32805$$
> 
> $$P(X \ge 2) = 1 - (0.59049 + 0.32805) = 1 - 0.91854 = 0.08146$$
> 
> **Answer:** (i) $0.0081$, (ii) $0.0815$.

### Problem 5: Defective Television Sets in Inspection Boxes (Slide Problem)
**Question:** A manufacturing process produces televisions where $10\%$ are defective ($p = 0.10$). If 4 television sets are randomly packaged into a box ($n = 4$):
1. What is the probability that a box contains no defective TVs?
2. What is the probability that a box contains at most 1 defective TV?
3. Out of 1,000 such boxes shipped to a distributor, how many boxes are expected to contain no defective TVs?

> [!note]- View Complete Solution
> Let $X$ be the number of defective TV sets in a box. $X \sim B(4, 0.10)$ with $q = 0.90$.
> 
> #### Part (i): No defective TVs ($X = 0$)
> $$P(X = 0) = \binom{4}{0} (0.10)^0 (0.90)^4 = (0.90)^4 = 0.6561$$
> 
> #### Part (ii): At most 1 defective TV ($X \le 1$)
> $$P(X \le 1) = P(X = 0) + P(X = 1)$$
> $$P(X = 1) = \binom{4}{1} (0.10)^1 (0.90)^3 = 4 \times 0.10 \times 0.729 = 0.2916$$
> $$P(X \le 1) = 0.6561 + 0.2916 = 0.9477$$
> 
> #### Part (iii): Expected boxes with no defectives out of $N = 1,000$
> $$\text{Expected Boxes} = N \times P(X = 0) = 1000 \times 0.6561 = 656.1 \approx 656 \text{ boxes}$$
> 
> **Answer:** (i) $0.6561$, (ii) $0.9477$, (iii) approximately $656$ boxes.

## 1.7 Parameter Constraints & Exam Diagnostic Rule

> [!error] Critical Exam Diagnostic: Binomial Variance vs Mean Rule
> For any valid Binomial distribution, because $0 < q < 1$:
> 
> $$V[X] = npq = (np)q < np = E[X]$$
> 
> **Rule:** In a Binomial distribution, **Variance MUST always be strictly less than Mean** ($V[X] < E[X]$).
> 
> **Exam Pitfall Case:** If an exam question asks to analyze a distribution given to have $\text{Mean} = 5$ and $\text{Standard Deviation} = 3$ ($\text{Variance} = 9$):
> - $np = 5$ and $npq = 9 \implies q = \frac{npq}{np} = \frac{9}{5} = 1.8$.
> - Since $q = 1.8 > 1$, this is **mathematically impossible** for a Binomial distribution!
