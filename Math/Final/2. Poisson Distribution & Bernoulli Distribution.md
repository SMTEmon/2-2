# 2. Poisson Distribution & Bernoulli Distribution

> [!abstract] Overview & Key Concepts
> This note covers two foundational discrete probability distributions:
> 1. **Bernoulli Distribution:** The building block for two-outcome single-trial experiments.
> 2. **Poisson Distribution (Law of Small Numbers):** Modeling rare events over continuous time or space intervals, complete with PMF verification proofs, mean/variance proofs, process characteristics, and step-by-step solved problems.

---

## 2.1 Bernoulli Distribution

### Definition & PMF
A random variable $X$ is said to have a **Bernoulli Distribution** with parameter $p$ ($0 \le p \le 1$) if $X$ can take only two possible values, $0$ and $1$, representing failure and success respectively.

The probability mass function (PMF) is:

$$f(x; p) = \begin{cases} p^x q^{1-x}, & x \in \{0, 1\} \\ 0, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

where $q = 1 - p$.

$$\begin{cases} P(X = 1) = p \\ P(X = 0) = 1 - p = q \end{cases}$$

Since the distribution involves only two cases of events, it is also known as a **two-point probability distribution**.

### Tabular & Graphical Representation

| $X = x$ | $P(X = x) = f(x; p)$ |
|---|---|
| **0** | $q = 1 - p$ |
| **1** | $p$ |

> [!note]- View Mathematical Derivations for Bernoulli Distribution
> #### Derivation of Mean $E[X]$
> $$E[X] = \sum_{x} x f(x; p) = 0 \cdot f(0; p) + 1 \cdot f(1; p) = 0 \cdot q + 1 \cdot p = p$$
> $$\therefore E[X] = p$$
> 
> #### Derivation of Variance $V[X]$
> $$V[X] = E[X^2] - (E[X])^2$$
> $$E[X^2] = \sum_{x} x^2 f(x; p) = 0^2 \cdot q + 1^2 \cdot p = p$$
> $$V[X] = p - p^2 = p(1 - p) = pq$$
> $$\therefore V[X] = pq = p(1 - p)$$

## 2.2 Poisson Distribution

### Definition & Poisson Process
The **Poisson Distribution**, also known as the **Law of Small Numbers**, is a discrete probability distribution named after Siméon Denis Poisson.

A random variable $X$ following this rule is referred to as a **Poisson Variate**, and the underlying process of generating values of such a random variable is known as a **Poisson Process**.

### Real-World Examples
1. Number of telephone calls received at a switchboard **per minute**.
2. Number of customers arriving at a bank counter **per 5-minute period**.
3. Number of machines breaking down during **one day**.
4. Number of drivers stopped at a roadblock not properly licensed **in a day**.
5. Number of days city schools remain closed due to heavy rain **during monsoon**.
6. Number of leakages in **100 km** of gas pipelines.

---

## 2.3 Probability Mass Function & Characteristics

Let $\mu$ be the mean or expected number of successes in a specified time interval or region. The probability distribution of the Poisson random variable $X$ is given by:

$$f(x; \mu) = \frac{e^{-\mu} \mu^x}{x!}, \quad x = 0, 1, 2, \dots, \infty$$

### Characteristics of a Poisson Distribution
1. **Interval Independence:** The number of successes occurring in one time interval is independent of those occurring in any other disjoint time interval.
2. **Proportionality:** The probability of a single success occurring during a very short time interval is proportional to the length of the time interval and does not depend on the number of successes occurring outside the interval.
3. **Negligible Multiple Occurrences:** The probability of more than one success occurring in such a short time interval is negligible.

## 2.4 PMF Verification & Mathematical Proofs

> [!note]- View Proof: Verification that $f(x; \mu)$ is a valid PMF ($\sum f(x) = 1$)
> **Theorem:** Verify that $f(x; \mu) = \frac{e^{-\mu} \mu^x}{x!}$ satisfies $\sum_{x=0}^{\infty} f(x; \mu) = 1$.
> 
> **Proof:**
> It is clear that $f(x; \mu) \ge 0$ for all $x \ge 0$.
> To verify that $\sum_{x=0}^{\infty} f(x; \mu) = 1$:
> 
> $$\sum_{x=0}^{\infty} f(x; \mu) = \sum_{x=0}^{\infty} \frac{e^{-\mu} \mu^x}{x!} = e^{-\mu} \sum_{x=0}^{\infty} \frac{\mu^x}{x!}$$
> 
> Recall the Maclaurin series expansion for $e^\mu$:
> 
> $$e^\mu = \sum_{x=0}^{\infty} \frac{\mu^x}{x!} = 1 + \frac{\mu}{1!} + \frac{\mu^2}{2!} + \frac{\mu^3}{3!} + \dots$$
> 
> Substituting this back:
> 
> $$\sum_{x=0}^{\infty} f(x; \mu) = e^{-\mu} \cdot e^\mu = e^0 = 1 \quad \text{(Proved)}$$

> [!note]- View Proof: Derivation of Mean $E[X] = \mu$
> **Proof:**
> 
> $$E[X] = \sum_{x=0}^{\infty} x f(x; \mu) = \sum_{x=0}^{\infty} x \frac{e^{-\mu} \mu^x}{x!}$$
> 
> Since the $x=0$ term vanishes:
> 
> $$E[X] = \sum_{x=1}^{\infty} x \frac{e^{-\mu} \mu^x}{x(x-1)!} = \sum_{x=1}^{\infty} \frac{e^{-\mu} \mu^x}{(x-1)!}$$
> 
> Factor out one $\mu$:
> 
> $$E[X] = \mu \sum_{x=1}^{\infty} \frac{e^{-\mu} \mu^{x-1}}{(x-1)!}$$
> 
> Substitute $y = x - 1$. As $x$ goes from $1$ to $\infty$, $y$ goes from $0$ to $\infty$:
> 
> $$E[X] = \mu \sum_{y=0}^{\infty} \frac{e^{-\mu} \mu^y}{y!} = \mu \sum_{y=0}^{\infty} f(y; \mu)$$
> 
> Since $\sum_{y=0}^{\infty} f(y; \mu) = 1$:
> 
> $$\therefore E[X] = \mu \cdot 1 = \mu$$

> [!note]- View Proof: Derivation of Variance $V[X] = \mu$
> **Proof:**
> Variance $V[X] = E[X^2] - (E[X])^2 = E[X(X-1)] + E[X] - \mu^2$.
> 
> First compute $E[X(X-1)]$:
> 
> $$E[X(X-1)] = \sum_{x=0}^{\infty} x(x-1) f(x; \mu) = \sum_{x=0}^{\infty} x(x-1) \frac{e^{-\mu} \mu^x}{x!}$$
> 
> The terms for $x=0$ and $x=1$ equal zero:
> 
> $$E[X(X-1)] = \sum_{x=2}^{\infty} x(x-1) \frac{e^{-\mu} \mu^x}{x(x-1)(x-2)!} = \sum_{x=2}^{\infty} \frac{e^{-\mu} \mu^x}{(x-2)!}$$
> 
> Factor out $\mu^2$:
> 
> $$E[X(X-1)] = \mu^2 \sum_{x=2}^{\infty} \frac{e^{-\mu} \mu^{x-2}}{(x-2)!}$$
> 
> Let $y = x - 2$:
> 
> $$E[X(X-1)] = \mu^2 \sum_{y=0}^{\infty} \frac{e^{-\mu} \mu^y}{y!} = \mu^2 \cdot 1 = \mu^2$$
> 
> Now, substitute into $E[X^2]$:
> 
> $$E[X^2] = E[X(X-1)] + E[X] = \mu^2 + \mu$$
> 
> Finally, compute Variance:
> 
> $$V[X] = E[X^2] - (E[X])^2 = (\mu^2 + \mu) - \mu^2 = \mu$$
> 
> $$\therefore V[X] = \mu$$

> [!important] Key Characteristic of Poisson Distribution
> For a Poisson distribution, **Mean equals Variance**:
> 
> $$E[X] = V[X] = \mu$$

## 2.5 Solved Numerical Problems

### Problem 1: Switchboard Call Rate
**Question:** Telephone calls arrive at a switchboard at a mean rate of $0.5$ calls/minute. Calculate the probability that exactly two calls will arrive in a particular **five-minute period**.

> [!note]- View Complete Solution
> Here, one time unit is $5$ minutes.
> The average number of calls per $5$-minute period is:
> 
> $$\mu = 0.5 \times 5 = 2.5$$
> 
> We need to compute $f(x=2; \mu=2.5)$:
> 
> $$f(2; 2.5) = \frac{e^{-2.5} (2.5)^2}{2!} = \frac{0.082085 \times 6.25}{2} = \frac{0.51303}{2} = 0.2565 \approx 0.257$$
> 
> **Answer:** The required probability is **$0.257$**.

### Problem 2: Telephone Operator Call Distribution
**Question:** The average number of calls received by a telephone operator during a $10$-minute interval from 5:00 PM to 5:10 PM daily is $3$. What is the probability that tomorrow during 5:00 PM to 5:10 PM the operator receives:
1. No calls?
2. Exactly one call?
3. At least two calls?

> [!note]- View Complete Solution
> Let $X$ be the random variable designating the number of calls received in the $10$-minute interval.
> $X \sim \text{Poisson}(\mu = 3)$.
> 
> #### Part (i): No calls ($X = 0$)
> $$P(X = 0) = f(0; 3) = \frac{e^{-3} 3^0}{0!} = e^{-3} = 0.0498$$
> 
> #### Part (ii): Exactly one call ($X = 1$)
> $$P(X = 1) = f(1; 3) = \frac{e^{-3} 3^1}{1!} = 3 e^{-3} = 3 \times 0.049787 = 0.1494$$
> 
> #### Part (iii): At least two calls ($X \ge 2$)
> $$P(X \ge 2) = 1 - P(X < 2) = 1 - \sum_{x=0}^{1} P(X = x) = 1 - [P(X = 0) + P(X = 1)]$$
> 
> $$P(X \ge 2) = 1 - [0.0498 + 0.1494] = 1 - 0.1992 = 0.8008$$
> 
> **Answer:**
> - (i) $0.0498$
> - (ii) $0.1494$
> - (iii) $0.8008$
