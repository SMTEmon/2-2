# 4. Normal Distribution & Chebyshev's Inequality

> [!abstract] Overview & Key Concepts
> This note covers continuous probability distributions, specifically focusing on **Chebyshev's Inequality** (empirical rule comparison, complete integral proof, and numerical example) and the **Normal Distribution** (PDF definition, full integration proofs for total area = 1, mean = $\mu$, variance = $\sigma^2$, standard normal $Z$-variate, CDF symmetry, and solved numerical problems).

## 4.1 Empirical Rule vs Chebyshev's Inequality

| Feature | Empirical Rule | Chebyshev's Inequality |
|---|---|---|
| **Applicability** | Applies **only** to symmetric, bell-shaped distributions. | Applies to **any** arbitrary probability distribution regardless of shape. |
| **$\mu \pm 1\sigma$ Interval** | Contains approximately **68%** of measurements. | No guarantee ($1 - 1/1^2 = 0$). |
| **$\mu \pm 2\sigma$ Interval** | Contains approximately **95%** of measurements. | At least **75%** ($1 - 1/2^2 = 3/4$). |
| **$\mu \pm 3\sigma$ Interval** | Contains approximately **99%** of measurements. | At least **88.89%** ($1 - 1/3^2 = 8/9$). |

## 4.2 Chebyshev's Theorem / Inequality

### Statement
If $\mu$ and $\sigma$ are the mean and standard deviation of a random variable $X$, then for any positive constant $k > 0$, the probability is **at least $1 - \frac{1}{k^2}$** that $X$ will take on a value within $k$ standard deviations of the mean:

$$P(|X - \mu| < k\sigma) \ge 1 - \frac{1}{k^2}$$

Equivalently, the tail probability of falling outside $k$ standard deviations is bounded by:

$$P(|X - \mu| \ge k\sigma) \le \frac{1}{k^2}$$

> [!note]- View Proof: Mathematical Proof of Chebyshev's Inequality
> **Proof:**
> By definition of variance for a continuous random variable $X$:
> 
> $$\sigma^2 = E[(X - \mu)^2] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} (x - \mu)^2 f(x) \, dx$$
> 
> Partition the integral into three sub-intervals: $(-\infty, \mu - k\sigma)$, $[\mu - k\sigma, \mu + k\sigma]$, and $(\mu + k\sigma, \infty)$:
> 
> $$\sigma^2 = \int_{-\infty}^{\mu - k\sigma} (x - \mu)^2 f(x) \, dx + \int_{\mu - k\sigma}^{\mu + k\sigma} (x - \mu)^2 f(x) \, dx + \int_{\mu + k\sigma}^{\infty} (x - \mu)^2 f(x) \, dx$$
> 
> Since the integrand $(x - \mu)^2 f(x)$ is non-negative everywhere, deleting the middle integral yields an inequality:
> 
> $$\sigma^2 \ge \int_{-\infty}^{\mu - k\sigma} (x - \mu)^2 f(x) \, dx + \int_{\mu + k\sigma}^{\infty} (x - \mu)^2 f(x) \, dx$$
> 
> For $x \le \mu - k\sigma$ or $x \ge \mu + k\sigma$, we have $|x - \mu| \ge k\sigma \implies (x - \mu)^2 \ge k^2 \sigma^2$.
> Replacing $(x - \mu)^2$ with lower bound $k^2 \sigma^2$:
> 
> $$\sigma^2 \ge \int_{-\infty}^{\mu - k\sigma} k^2 \sigma^2 f(x) \, dx + \int_{\mu + k\sigma}^{\infty} k^2 \sigma^2 f(x) \, dx$$
> 
> Factoring out $k^2 \sigma^2$ and dividing both sides by $k^2 \sigma^2$:
> 
> $$\frac{1}{k^2} \ge \int_{-\infty}^{\mu - k\sigma} f(x) \, dx + \int_{\mu + k\sigma}^{\infty} f(x) \, dx$$
> 
> Notice that the right side represents the tail probability $P(|X - \mu| \ge k\sigma)$:
> 
> $$P(|X - \mu| \ge k\sigma) \le \frac{1}{k^2}$$
> 
> Taking the complement $P(|X - \mu| < k\sigma) = 1 - P(|X - \mu| \ge k\sigma)$:
> 
> $$P(|X - \mu| < k\sigma) \ge 1 - \frac{1}{k^2} \quad \text{(Proved)}$$

### Solved Chebyshev Problem
**Question:** If the probability density function of $X$ is given by:

$$f(x) = \begin{cases} 630 x^4 (1-x)^4, & 0 < x < 1 \\ 0, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

Find the probability that $X$ will take on a value within two standard deviations ($k = 2$) of the mean, and compare this probability with the lower bound provided by Chebyshev's Inequality.

> [!note]- View Complete Solution
> **Step 1: Compute Mean $\mu = E[X]$**
> $$\mu = \int_{0}^{1} x \cdot 630 x^4 (1-x)^4 \, dx = 630 \int_{0}^{1} x^5 (1-x)^4 \, dx$$
> Using the Beta function $B(m, n) = \int_0^1 x^{m-1} (1-x)^{n-1} dx = \frac{\Gamma(m)\Gamma(n)}{\Gamma(m+n)}$:
> $$\mu = 630 \cdot B(6, 5) = 630 \cdot \frac{5! \cdot 4!}{10!} = 630 \cdot \frac{120 \cdot 24}{3,628,800} = \frac{1}{2} = 0.5$$
> 
> **Step 2: Compute Variance $\sigma^2 = V[X]$**
> $$E[X^2] = 630 \int_{0}^{1} x^6 (1-x)^4 \, dx = 630 \cdot B(7, 5) = 630 \cdot \frac{6! \cdot 4!}{11!} = 0.2727$$
> $$\sigma^2 = V[X] = E[X^2] - \mu^2 = 0.2727 - (0.5)^2 = 0.2727 - 0.25 = \frac{1}{44} \approx 0.022727$$
> $$\sigma = \sqrt{\frac{1}{44}} \approx 0.15$$
> 
> **Step 3: Calculate Interval Bounds for $k = 2$**
> - Lower bound: $\mu - 2\sigma = 0.5 - 2(0.15) = 0.20$
> - Upper bound: $\mu + 2\sigma = 0.5 + 2(0.15) = 0.80$
> 
> **Step 4: Exact Probability Integration**
> $$P(0.20 < X < 0.80) = \int_{0.2}^{0.8} 630 x^4 (1-x)^4 \, dx = 0.96$$
> 
> **Step 5: Chebyshev Lower Bound**
> $$P(|X - \mu| < 2\sigma) \ge 1 - \frac{1}{2^2} = 1 - \frac{1}{4} = 0.75$$
> 
> **Conclusion:** The exact probability ($0.96$) is significantly higher and stronger than the conservative lower bound ($0.75$) provided by Chebyshev's inequality.

## 4.3 Normal Probability Distribution

### Definition & PDF
A continuous random variable $X$ having a bell-shaped distribution is called a **Normal Random Variable**, denoted as $X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)$.

The Probability Density Function (PDF) is given by:

$$f(x; \mu, \sigma^2) = \frac{1}{\sigma \sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\left[ -\frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma} \right)^2 \right], \quad -\infty < x < \infty$$

where parameters $\mu \in (-\infty, \infty)$ is the mean and $\sigma^2 > 0$ is the variance.

---

## 4.4 Fundamental Derivations for Normal Distribution

> [!note]- View Proof 1: Total Area under Normal Curve equals 1
> **Theorem:** Prove that $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x; \mu, \sigma^2) \, dx = 1$.
> 
> **Proof:**
> 
> $$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{1}{\sigma \sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\left[ -\frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma} \right)^2 \right] dx$$
> 
> Substitute $y = \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right)^2 \implies \sqrt{2y} = \frac{x-\mu}{\sigma} \implies x = \mu + \sigma \sqrt{2y}$.
> Differentiating: $dx = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{2y}} dy$.
> 
> Substituting limits and variables:
> 
> $$I = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x) dx = 2 \int_{0}^{\infty} \frac{1}{\sigma \sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-y} \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{2y}} dy = \frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi} \sqrt{2}} \int_{0}^{\infty} y^{-1/2} e^{-y} dy$$
> $$I = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}} \int_{0}^{\infty} y^{\frac{1}{2} - 1} e^{-y} dy$$
> 
> Recall Gamma function definition $\Gamma(n) = \int_0^\infty y^{n-1} e^{-y} dy$ and $\Gamma(1/2) = \sqrt{\pi}$:
> 
> $$I = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}} \cdot \Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}} \cdot \sqrt{\pi} = 1 \quad \text{(Proved)}$$

> [!note]- View Proof 2: Derivation of Mean $E[X] = \mu$
> **Proof:**
> 
> $$E[X] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} x \cdot \frac{1}{\sigma \sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\left[ -\frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma} \right)^2 \right] dx$$
> 
> Let $y = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma} \implies x = \sigma y + \mu \implies dx = \sigma dy$.
> 
> $$E[X] = \frac{1}{\sigma \sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} (\sigma y + \mu) e^{-\frac{1}{2} y^2} \sigma dy = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} (\sigma y + \mu) e^{-\frac{1}{2} y^2} dy$$
> $$E[X] = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} y e^{-\frac{1}{2} y^2} dy + \frac{\mu}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-\frac{1}{2} y^2} dy$$
> 
> - The first integrand $y e^{-\frac{1}{2} y^2}$ is an **odd function**, so its integral over symmetric limits $(-\infty, \infty)$ is **$0$**.
> - The second integral $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-\frac{1}{2} y^2} dy = 1$ (standard normal area).
> 
> $$E[X] = 0 + \mu \cdot 1 = \mu \quad \text{(Proved)}$$

> [!note]- View Proof 3: Derivation of Variance $V[X] = \sigma^2$
> **Proof:**
> $$V[X] = E[(X - \mu)^2] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} (x - \mu)^2 \frac{1}{\sigma \sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\left[ -\frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma} \right)^2 \right] dx$$
> 
> Substitute $y = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma} \implies dx = \sigma dy$:
> 
> $$V[X] = \frac{1}{\sigma \sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} (\sigma y)^2 e^{-\frac{1}{2} y^2} \sigma dy = \frac{\sigma^2}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} y^2 e^{-\frac{1}{2} y^2} dy$$
> 
> By symmetry, rewrite as $2 \times$ integral from $0$ to $\infty$:
> 
> $$V[X] = \frac{2\sigma^2}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{0}^{\infty} y^2 e^{-\frac{1}{2} y^2} dy$$
> 
> Substitute $z = \frac{1}{2} y^2 \implies y = \sqrt{2z} \implies dy = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2z}} dz$:
> 
> $$V[X] = \frac{2\sigma^2}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{0}^{\infty} (2z) e^{-z} \frac{1}{\sqrt{2z}} dz = \frac{2\sigma^2}{\sqrt{\pi}} \int_{0}^{\infty} z^{1/2} e^{-z} dz$$
> $$V[X] = \frac{2\sigma^2}{\sqrt{\pi}} \int_{0}^{\infty} z^{\frac{3}{2} - 1} e^{-z} dz = \frac{2\sigma^2}{\sqrt{\pi}} \Gamma\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)$$
> 
> Using $\Gamma(3/2) = \frac{1}{2} \Gamma(1/2) = \frac{1}{2} \sqrt{\pi}$:
> 
> $$V[X] = \frac{2\sigma^2}{\sqrt{\pi}} \cdot \frac{1}{2} \sqrt{\pi} = \sigma^2 \quad \text{(Proved)}$$

## 4.5 Standard Normal Distribution ($Z$-variate)

### Definition
If $X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)$, then the transformed variable:

$$Z = \frac{X - \mu}{\sigma}$$

is called the **Standard Normal Variate** or **$Z$-score**, distributed as $Z \sim N(0, 1)$.

The standard normal density function is:

$$f(z; 0, 1) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\left[ -\frac{1}{2} z^2 \right], \quad -\infty < z < \infty$$

> [!note]- View Proof: $E[Z] = 0$ and $V[Z] = 1$
> $$E[Z] = E\left[\frac{X - \mu}{\sigma}\right] = \frac{1}{\sigma} (E[X] - \mu) = \frac{1}{\sigma} (\mu - \mu) = 0$$
> 
> $$V[Z] = V\left[\frac{X - \mu}{\sigma}\right] = \frac{1}{\sigma^2} V[X - \mu] = \frac{1}{\sigma^2} V[X] = \frac{\sigma^2}{\sigma^2} = 1$$

---

## 4.6 Cumulative Distribution Function (CDF) & Symmetry

The CDF of the standard normal distribution is denoted by $\Phi(z)$:

$$\Phi(z) = P(Z \le z) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{-\infty}^{z} e^{-t^2/2} \, dt$$

### Symmetry Property
Because the standard normal PDF is symmetric about $z = 0$:

$$P(Z \le z) = P(Z \ge -z) \implies \Phi(z) + \Phi(-z) = 1$$

$$\implies P(Z \ge -z) = 1 - \Phi(-z) = \Phi(z)$$

## 4.7 Solved Numerical Problems

### Problem 1: Central Area of Normal Distribution
**Question:** In a normal distribution with mean $\mu = 50$ and variance $\sigma^2 = 100$ ($\sigma = 10$), find the values of $X$ that cover the central $50\%$ of the area under the curve.

> [!note]- View Complete Solution
> We need to find bounds $k_1$ and $k_2$ such that $P(-k_1 < Z < k_2) = 0.50$.
> By symmetry, $k_1 = k_2 = k$, so:
> 
> $$P(-k < Z < k) = 2 P(0 < Z < k) = 0.50 \implies P(0 < Z < k) = 0.25$$
> 
> From standard normal Z-tables, the $z$-value corresponding to an area of $0.25$ above the mean is:
> 
> $$k = 0.599$$
> 
> Now transform back to $X$ using $x = \mu + z \sigma$:
> - $x_1 = \mu - k \sigma = 50 - (0.599)(10) = 50 - 5.99 = 44.01$
> - $x_2 = \mu + k \sigma = 50 + (0.599)(10) = 50 + 5.99 = 55.99$
> 
> **Answer:** The central $50\%$ of the area is covered by the interval **$(44.01, 55.99)$**, i.e., $P(44.01 < X < 55.99) = 0.50$.

### Problem 2: Unknown Variance Determination
**Question:** A normal random variable $X$ has mean $\mu = 30$ and unknown variance $\sigma^2$. If $P(X < 35) = 0.69$, find the variance of $X$.

> [!note]- View Complete Solution
> Standardize the variable:
> 
> $$P(X < 35) = P\left( Z < \frac{35 - 30}{\sigma} \right) = P\left( Z < \frac{5}{\sigma} \right) = 0.69$$
> 
> From standard normal Z-tables, $P(Z < 0.5) = 0.69$.
> Equating the Z-scores:
> 
> $$\frac{5}{\sigma} = 0.5 \implies \sigma = \frac{5}{0.5} = 10$$
> 
> Variance $\sigma^2 = 10^2 = 100$.
> 
> **Answer:** The variance of $X$ is **$\sigma^2 = 100$**.
