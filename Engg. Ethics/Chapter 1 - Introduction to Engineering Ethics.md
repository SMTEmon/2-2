### 1️⃣ Core Concepts: What Are We Actually Studying?

Engineering ethics starts when a technical decision changes what happens to other people and creates a duty to answer for it.

*   **Ethics:** Moral judgment about how people *ought* to act toward others.
*   **Engineering Ethics:** The rules and standards governing the conduct of engineers in their role as professionals. It is the professional judgment used inside technical work, institutions, and public risk management. 

#### 🧠 Moral Autonomy Under Pressure
**Moral autonomy** is the ability to think critically and independently about moral issues and to apply this moral thinking to situations that arise in the course of professional engineering practice.

Professional ethics begins where *passive obedience stops*. Spotting obvious right and wrong is not the hard part; the hard part is keeping your judgment intact when surrounded by conflicting pressures:
*   Employer targets (cost, schedule)
*   Public safety
*   Law and policy
*   Team culture and authority

#### ⚠️ Why Good Intentions Are Not Enough
Many failures begin with competent people making decisions that feel practical at the time. However, good intentions do not cancel out weak judgment, weak processes, or weak resistance to pressure.
The cycle of ethical failure often looks like this:
`Incomplete Data ➔ Schedule Pressure ➔ Normalizing Risk ➔ Ethical Failure`

### 2️⃣ Key Distinctions & Frameworks

#### Personal Ethics vs. Professional Ethics
There is a distinct boundary between the ethics we practice at home and those we practice at work. Professional ethics often involve choices on an organizational level (e.g., corporation vs. government).

| Dimension | Personal Ethics | Professional Ethics |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Focus** | Everyday conduct with other people. | Duties inside a formal role with public consequences. |
| **Setting** | Private and social life. | Employer, regulator, client, and public. |
| **Pressure** | Mostly personal. | Institutional, legal, economic, and technical. |
| **Stakes** | Limited local harm. | Large-scale harm, system failure, loss of trust. |

#### ⚖️ Ethics and the Law
The practice of engineering is governed by many laws, but **what is legal is not always ethical**. 

*   **Legal but unethical:** A product can meet the letter of the law but still expose people to avoidable danger (e.g., releasing a known, but *unregulated*, toxic substance).
*   **Illegal but ethical:** Law can lag behind technical evidence. A substance might be banned based on outdated science, but perfectly safe to use based on modern data.
*   **Takeaway:** Law sets a floor. Ethics matters most when the law is silent, late, narrow, or technically shallow.

### 3️⃣ The Nature of Engineering

#### Managing the Unknown (Burden of Proof)
Engineering design is about creating new devices. Therefore, an engineer's job is largely to *manage the unknown*. You can never be absolutely certain a design won't harm anyone, but you must ask:
1. *How well will this work outside ideal conditions?*
2. *Who carries the risk if our estimate is wrong?*

When evidence is incomplete, you must decide whether to **proceed now** (public absorbs the failure risk) or **pause for better evidence** (firm absorbs delay and cost). Uncertainty does not remove responsibility.

> **Good Practice for the Unknown:**
> ✔️ Test seriously & document uncertainty.
> ✔️ Anticipate failure.
> ❌ Resist fake certainty.

#### 🛠️ Ethics Problems Behave Like Design Problems
Engineering ethics problems are open-ended and rarely have one single correct formulaic answer, much like engineering design.

| Why the comparison matters | What does *not* follow |
| :--- | :--- |
| Both involve constraints, trade-offs, and incomplete information. | Ambiguity does *not* mean "anything goes." |
| Both require judgment rather than a single mechanical answer. | Disagreement does *not* make all positions equally defensible. |
| Both allow for stronger and weaker responses. | Uncertainty increases the need for judgment; it does *not* remove it. |

### 4️⃣ Case Studies in Engineering Ethics

#### 🚗 The Ford Pinto Case (1978)
*   **The Conflict:** Ford engineers designed the Pinto with a rear fuel tank vulnerability to meet a price and weight target for the subcompact market. 
*   **The Outcome:** Rear-end collisions caused the tank to rupture and burst into flames. In 1978, three teenage girls died in Indiana, leading to **criminal charges** against Ford (a massive departure from standard civil lawsuits).
*   **The Ethical Force:** The ethical problem wasn't just the crash; it was that the *design vulnerability was already known*. Once a hazard is known, every additional shipment is an active decision about who absorbs the risk.

#### 🚀 Space Shuttle Challenger (1986)
*   **Background:** The Shuttle used Solid Rocket Boosters (SRBs) built by Morton Thiokol. The joints were sealed by rubber O-rings. 
*   **The Technical Flaw:** When ignited, the booster walls expanded ("joint rotation"), pulling the joint apart. The O-rings were meant to seal this gap, but cold temperatures made the rubber brittle and unable to seal properly.
*   **The Pressure:** NASA faced immense political pressure to launch frequently and affordably. There were delays, and the launch was scheduled during a freezing Florida morning ($8^\circ \text{F}$ overnight, previously never launched below $53^\circ \text{F}$).
*   **The Conflict:** Thiokol engineers (Roger Boisjoly, Arnie Thompson) argued aggressively *against* launching due to cold-weather O-ring data. NASA managers pushed back because the data wasn't perfectly conclusive.
*   **The Fatal Quote:** Under pressure to secure future contracts, Thiokol General Manager Jerald Mason told VP of Engineering Bob Lund: *"Take off your engineering hat and put on your management hat."* Lund reversed the safety recommendation.
*   **The Result:** The O-rings failed, hot gas escaped, and the shuttle exploded, killing all 7 astronauts.
*   **Takeaway:** The strongest warning was not lack of data. It was the choice to treat incomplete data as permission to proceed. 

**Challenger "Who's Who" Quick Reference**

| Organization/Person | Role |
| :--- | :--- |
| **NASA** (Larry Mulloy) | Marshall SRB Project Manager (pushed for launch) |

#### ☄️ Space Shuttle Columbia (2003)
*   **The Incident:** During liftoff, a piece of insulating foam broke off the fuel tank and struck the left wing at >500 mph, compromising the heat-shielding ceramic tiles. Upon reentry 16 days later, the wing melted and the shuttle disintegrated.
*   **The Ethical Failure (Normalization of Deviance):** Foam strikes had happened on numerous previous launches without destroying the shuttle. Because they survived before, NASA management started treating the hazard as normal. 
*   **Takeaway:** Repeated survival can create false confidence. Visible danger can slowly become routine. One tragedy (Challenger) does not automatically fix an organization's safety culture.

### 5️⃣ Actionable Takeaways: When Advice is Ignored

If you raise a safety concern and management overrules you, the problem shifts. The question is no longer just whether the design is safe, but **how far your professional responsibility extends after disagreement**.

If overruled, an ethical engineer should:
1.  **Document** the concern clearly (in writing).
2.  **Escalate** through the available chain of command.
3.  **Refuse** direct participation in the work if necessary.
4.  **Consider outside disclosure** (whistleblowing) *only* when the risk is serious and internal channels have completely failed.
